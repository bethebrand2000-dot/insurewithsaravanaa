Stories

Why insurance matters, told simply.

Four situations families commonly face. They are here so you can ask the right questions before you need the answers.

Please note: these stories are imagined to illustrate common situations. Names, places and details are not real, and they are not cases of actual clients.
Illustrative story

The bill nobody planned for

A shopkeeper in his forties gets a phone call at night: his father has chest pain. Within hours there is a hospital, a procedure and a bill.

Ravi (name imagined) runs a small shop. He had a health policy and never thought about it again. At the hospital he learns that the plan caps the room rent, and that choosing a better room reduces other payments in proportion. The final claim pays far less than he expected.

Nobody lied to him and nobody was careless. He simply never had a chance to ask the right questions before the night he needed the answers.

What it teaches: Before you buy, ask about room-rent limits, sub-limits and co-payment. They decide how much of the bill you will pay yourself.
Illustrative story

One family, two hospital stays in a year

A family of four is covered by a single family floater. A surgery in the spring is followed by a child's illness in the winter.

Meena and her husband chose a floater because it looked economical. When her surgery was paid for, most of the shared sum insured was used up. Months later their son needed admission, and what remained was thin.

A floater can be an excellent choice. The question is whether the cover is sized for a bad year, not an average one.

What it teaches: Think about the worst year, not the typical year. Ask how the sum insured is shared and whether it can be topped up.
Illustrative story

At fifty-eight, with nothing in place

A retired couple were always told they would sort out health cover later. Later arrived with a diagnosis.

Lakshmi and her husband relied on one employer plan. When he retired, it ended. They looked for cover for the first time in their late fifties, and found that premiums were higher and certain conditions would be covered only after a waiting period.

They are still insured today. But they paid more than they would have, and some of their early years were not covered for what worried them most.

What it teaches: The best time to start is when you are healthy. The second best is today, with honest answers about your health.
Illustrative story

The claim that nearly failed

A man with mild diabetes bought a policy and left it off the form, assuming it was too minor to matter.

Years later, an admission related to his condition led the insurer to question the original application. The claim became long and stressful for the family.

It is a story about disclosure. Telling the insurer everything at the start can mean higher premiums or a waiting period. Not telling can mean the claim you rely on is disputed.

What it teaches: Declare everything, even if it seems small. A clear start protects the claim you may one day depend on.
